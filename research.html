Research

Rain is a big part of what makes a tropical cyclone dangerous.

We often talk about cyclones in terms of wind speed and category. But for many people, the worst harm comes from heavy rain and flooding. Two storms with the same wind speed can bring very different rain, and where that rain falls matters just as much as how much.

I study one simple question: where does rain fall around a cyclone, and why? Some storms have rain wrapped evenly around the center. Others have rain pushed to one side or stretched far out from the eye. I look at the shape and size of this rainfall structure and try to understand what environmental conditions is associated with its pattern.

I focus on where the rain falls and how that is associated with environmental conditions

I am also interested in how rainfall changes as a storm moves toward land and crosses the coast, since that is when people on the ground feel the effects most.

Much of this starts with a practical problem: we rely on satellite data to see rainfall over the ocean and land, and different datasets do not always agree. Part of my work is understanding the dataset advantanges and disadvantages in the context of tropical cyclone rainfall.

My dissertation is called Spatial Analysis of Precipitation Patterns Associated with Tropical Cyclones. My advisor is Dr. Corene Matyas at the University of Florida.

Tools I use

Data: IMERG, MSWEP, ERA5, TC PRIMED, IBTrACS

Code: Python (xarray, pandas, matplotlib), R, NetCDF

Mapping: ArcGIS Pro, QGIS, OpenStreetMap tools

Earlier work

Before my PhD, I worked on cyclone risk from a community point of view. As a YouthMappers Research Fellow, I built a native-language mapping and evacuation plan for women in coastal Bangladesh. That experience is what first pulled me toward Tropical Cyclone induced Rainfall research.
